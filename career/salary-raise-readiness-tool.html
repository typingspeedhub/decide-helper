What this salary raise tool does

A pay raise request is easier to prepare when you separate the number from the case behind the number. This tool combines a simple salary increase calculator with a readiness checklist. It calculates the raise amount and raise percentage you are considering, shows how your current and target salary compare with a market benchmark, and scores the preparation factors that can make a compensation discussion more specific.

The readiness score does not predict whether your employer will approve a raise. Instead, it helps identify weak areas—such as limited market research, unclear achievements, poor timing, or an undefined request—that you may want to strengthen before the meeting.

      Rerun the tool with a lower market benchmark, weaker timing, or a smaller assessment of your results. If your readiness depends on one uncertain assumption, verify that assumption before relying on it in a salary negotiation.

      How the Salary Raise Readiness Tool works

      The tool separates two questions: What salary increase are you considering? and How prepared are you to support that request?

      The salary calculator portion compares your current salary with your target salary and, when provided, a market-pay benchmark. The readiness portion scores evidence, performance, responsibility growth, salary research, timing, company context, process knowledge, and negotiation preparation. The final score is a preparation indicator, not a prediction of approval.

      How to calculate a raise percentage

      A raise percentage calculator converts the difference between your current salary and your target salary into a percentage of current pay.

      Raise percentage = ((target salary − current salary) ÷ current salary) × 100

      Example: if your current salary is 50,000 and your target is 55,000, the requested increase is 5,000. Dividing 5,000 by 50,000 gives 0.10, or a 10% raise.

      The percentage is useful for comparison, but it is not the argument by itself. A salary increase request is usually stronger when the number is connected to market pay, role scope, results, skills, and the employer's compensation process.

      How much of a raise should I ask for?

      There is no single raise percentage that is right for every employee. A routine annual adjustment, a promotion, a market correction, and a major increase in responsibilities are different situations. Instead of selecting an arbitrary percentage, build a request from evidence.

      Factor What to examine Why it matters
      Market salary Comparable roles, location, seniority, industry, and responsibilities Helps show whether your current pay is below, near, or above a relevant market range.
      Role scope New projects, clients, systems, decisions, leadership, or responsibilities A materially larger role can support a compensation review.
      Results Revenue, savings, quality, speed, output, reliability, customer outcomes, or risk reduction Specific achievements make your contribution easier to explain.
      Skills New technical, language, leadership, certification, or specialist capabilities Relevant skills may increase the value and difficulty of replacing your work.
      Internal context Pay bands, review cycles, promotion process, budget timing, and company conditions A reasonable request still has to move through the employer's actual process.

      Use a defensible target, not a random number

      If your market research suggests a range, compare that range with your current salary and the scope of your actual work. Your target should be a number you can explain—not simply the largest percentage you think might be accepted.

      What makes you ready to ask for a raise?

      Readiness is less about confidence and more about preparation. A manager may need to explain or defend your salary increase request to HR, finance, or another decision-maker. A concise, evidence-based case is easier to carry through that process.

      1. Define what changed. Identify how your responsibilities, performance, skills, or market position differ from when your salary was last set.
      2. Collect three to five strong examples. Use specific results or expanded responsibilities instead of general claims that you work hard.
      3. Research your market value. Use several relevant salary sources when possible and compare like with like.
      4. Choose a salary target. Know the salary level or range you want to discuss and why it is defensible.
      5. Check timing and process. Learn when compensation decisions are made and who needs to approve them.
      6. Practice the conversation. Prepare a short opening, then be ready to listen rather than over-explain.
      7. Plan for more than yes or no. Know what questions to ask if the answer is “not now” and which alternatives would matter to you.

      How to research your salary market value

      Salary research can strengthen a raise request, but only when the comparison is relevant. A broad national average for a job title can be misleading if the roles differ in location, seniority, industry, shift pattern, company size, languages, technical requirements, or responsibilities.

      Useful sources can include:

      • current job advertisements with salary ranges;
      • government or official labor statistics;
      • recruiter salary guides;
      • professional associations;
      • reputable salary databases;
      • recruiters familiar with your role and market.

      Try to identify a reasonable range rather than one precise “correct” salary. If different sources disagree, note the differences instead of choosing only the highest figure.

      When is the best time to ask for a raise?

      Good timing cannot replace a weak case, but it can affect whether the employer is able to act. A salary discussion may be easier to evaluate after a strong achievement, after you take on substantially more responsibility, during a performance-review cycle, or before annual pay budgets are finalized.

      Timing may be less favorable during severe budget cuts, announced layoffs, a compensation freeze, unresolved performance concerns, or immediately after you have already received a significant adjustment. Even then, documenting your contribution and asking about the future review process can provide useful information.

      How to ask for a raise professionally

      A raise conversation does not need to be confrontational. Keep the discussion centered on the value and scope of your work, the evidence you have collected, and the salary adjustment you want to discuss.

      A simple structure for the conversation

      Open by saying you would like to discuss compensation. Summarize two or three strong changes or achievements. State the salary level or range you want to discuss and the evidence behind it. Then stop and give your manager room to respond.

      If you want help drafting a concise meeting request or follow-up message, Reply Helper can be used as a writing aid. Edit any draft so it accurately reflects your role, achievements, and workplace.

      What if your raise request is denied?

      A rejection can mean different things: insufficient budget, poor timing, a weak business case, a salary-band limit, company policy, or a performance requirement. The reason matters more than the word “no” by itself.

      Useful follow-up questions include:

      • What specifically would need to change for an increase to become possible?
      • When can compensation realistically be reviewed again?
      • Who needs to approve the adjustment?
      • Are there measurable goals that would support a future increase?
      • Can the expectations and review date be documented?
      • Are there alternatives such as a bonus, additional leave, flexibility, training, or a formal role-level review?

      A specific future date and measurable conditions give you more useful information than an indefinite promise to “revisit it later.”

      Common salary negotiation mistakes

      • Choosing a raise percentage without researching comparable pay.
      • Building the argument mainly around personal expenses instead of role value.
      • Saying you “deserve more” without specific achievements or scope changes.
      • Using one salary website as if it proves the exact value of your role.
      • Comparing yourself aggressively with a coworker whose full compensation or responsibilities you may not know.
      • Asking without knowing the salary target or range you want to discuss.
      • Ignoring the employer's compensation cycle and approval process.
      • Using a fake job offer as leverage.
      • Threatening to quit before deciding whether you would actually leave.
      • Accepting a vague “maybe later” without asking for criteria and timing.

      Frequently asked questions

      How do I calculate my raise percentage?

      Subtract your current salary from your target salary. Divide the increase by your current salary and multiply by 100. The calculator above performs the calculation automatically.

      How much of a raise should I ask for?

      There is no universal percentage. Compare your current pay with relevant market data, responsibility growth, results, performance, skills, internal salary structure, and company conditions. A defensible request is more useful than a generic percentage.

      Can I use this as a pay raise calculator?

      Yes. Enter your current salary and target salary to calculate the requested increase and raise percentage. Add a market salary benchmark if you also want to compare your pay with an estimated market level.

      What is a good reason to ask for a salary increase?

      Strong reasons can include materially expanded responsibilities, sustained strong performance, measurable achievements, valuable new skills, operating at a higher role level, or credible evidence that your pay is below the market range for comparable work.

      When is the best time to ask for a raise?

      Useful timing may follow a major achievement, expanded responsibilities, a strong review, or the period before pay budgets are finalized. Learn your employer's compensation cycle where possible.

      Should I ask for a raise by email?

      A short email or message can be useful for requesting a dedicated meeting. The actual salary negotiation is often easier in a live conversation when practical, although workplace norms and remote arrangements can affect the best format.

      What if my salary is below market rate?

      Verify that the benchmark is genuinely comparable, then use the market gap as one part of your case. Combine it with your responsibilities, results, performance, and internal role level rather than relying on the market number alone.

      Does a high readiness score mean I will get the raise?

      No. The score reflects the preparation factors you enter. It cannot know your employer's budget, salary bands, approval process, management judgment, or future business conditions.
