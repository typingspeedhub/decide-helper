What this calculator compares

This tool does more than compare rent with a mortgage payment. It models the cash cost of renting, the cash cost of ownership, the mortgage balance after your chosen time period, estimated home value, selling costs, and the equity you may keep when you sell.

It also gives you an optional opportunity-cost estimate for the cash a renter may keep available instead of using it for a down payment and purchase costs. The result is an estimate, not a prediction of future home prices, rent increases, investment returns, or mortgage costs.

      Do not treat this result as a forecast

      Small changes in home appreciation, rent growth, mortgage rates, maintenance, taxes, insurance, and selling costs can materially change the result. Run several scenarios, including a conservative case with lower appreciation and higher repair costs.

      How the rent vs buy calculator works

      The calculator estimates the economic cost of each housing choice over the number of years you expect to stay.

      For renting, it adds rent, renter-only costs, and rent increases. If you choose to include opportunity cost, it also estimates the future value of the upfront cash a renter keeps instead of using it for a down payment and purchase costs. When renting is cheaper in a given month, the model can also add that positive difference to the renter's hypothetical investment balance.

      For buying, it calculates the mortgage payment, mortgage interest and principal, taxes, insurance, maintenance, fees, purchase costs, and other owner-only costs. At the end of the selected period, it estimates the future home value, subtracts the remaining mortgage and selling costs, and treats the remaining equity as value retained by the owner.

      Economic cost is different from cash paid

      Mortgage principal is cash leaving your bank account, but it can become equity rather than a permanent expense. That is why the calculator separately shows owner cash outflow and the estimated equity remaining after a sale.

      Why comparing rent with the mortgage payment can be misleading

      A mortgage payment usually contains principal and interest, but homeowners may also pay property taxes, homeowners insurance, maintenance, repairs, association fees, and transaction costs. These additional costs can materially change the comparison.

      Mortgage payment formula used by the tool

      For a standard fixed-payment mortgage, the calculator uses the common amortizing-loan formula. If the mortgage rate is zero, it simply divides the loan amount by the number of monthly payments.

      What the mortgage payment includes

      The mortgage payment shown in the results is principal plus interest only. Property tax, insurance, maintenance, HOA or building fees, and other owner costs are added separately so you can see the full picture.

      Why time horizon matters so much

      Buying and selling can involve meaningful one-time costs. A short holding period gives those costs fewer years to spread across. It may also leave less time for mortgage principal repayment or home appreciation to offset purchase and selling expenses.

      There is no universal five-year or seven-year rule. The break-even period depends on your actual local numbers. A market with low transaction costs and expensive rent can favor buying sooner, while a high-cost market with moderate rent can favor renting for much longer.

      How to choose a home appreciation assumption

      Future property values are uncertain. Avoid using recent short-term price growth as a guaranteed long-term return. A useful approach is to run the calculator several times: once with conservative appreciation, once with a middle estimate, and once with a stronger scenario.

      Stress-test the decision

      If buying only wins when you assume unusually strong appreciation, low maintenance, and rising rent, the decision is more fragile. A stronger purchase remains reasonable under less favorable assumptions.

      Opportunity cost of the down payment

      A down payment is not the same as a fee because it becomes part of your home equity. However, it ties up cash that could otherwise stay liquid or be invested. The calculator therefore lets you include an estimated return on the renter's available cash.

      This assumption should be used carefully. Investment returns are uncertain, taxes may apply, and many renters do not actually invest the difference. If you would spend the cash rather than save it, choose zero percent or switch off monthly renter-savings investing.

      Maintenance is not a fixed universal percentage

      The maintenance percentage is only a planning estimate. An older detached house, a new apartment, a recently renovated property, and a home with aging roof, plumbing, or heating systems can have very different future repair needs.

      Use a number that reflects the specific property where possible. You can also rerun the tool with a higher maintenance assumption to see how sensitive the result is to future repairs.

      What the estimated break-even year means

      The calculator checks the modeled rent and buy cost at the end of each year up to your selected time horizon. If the result changes from renting being cheaper to buying being cheaper, it reports the first approximate year when buying becomes the lower-cost modeled option.

      This is not a guaranteed real-world break-even date. It is only the point at which the assumptions entered into this model produce a crossover.

      Financial factors the calculator cannot fully capture

      • Local tax deductions or housing subsidies.
      • Private mortgage insurance or country-specific mortgage insurance systems.
      • Variable-rate mortgage resets and refinancing decisions.
      • Large one-time renovations or unusual repair events.
      • Special assessments from a building or homeowners association.
      • Capital-gains taxes or exemptions when selling.
      • Local tenant protections, rent controls, and lease rules.
      • Moving costs that differ substantially between renting and buying.
      • The value of your time spent maintaining a property.
      • Differences in commute, utilities, size, and neighborhood quality between the homes compared.

      Lifestyle factors still matter

      A financially cheaper option is not automatically the better life decision. Renting may offer mobility, lower maintenance responsibility, and easier relocation. Buying may provide control, stability, privacy, and the ability to modify the property.

      If one option is only slightly cheaper in the calculator, personal factors may reasonably dominate the decision. A small modeled difference is not enough to ignore job flexibility, family plans, school needs, commute, neighborhood preferences, or your tolerance for repair risk.

      A practical seven-step rent vs buy process

      1. Estimate how long you may stay. Use a realistic time horizon rather than your ideal plan.
      2. Compare similar homes. Match size, location, commute, condition, and amenities as closely as possible.
      3. Use the full ownership cost. Include taxes, insurance, maintenance, fees, and transaction costs.
      4. Choose conservative assumptions. Do not depend on rapid appreciation or unusually low maintenance.
      5. Run several scenarios. Change rent growth, home appreciation, mortgage rate, and maintenance.
      6. Check financial resilience. Make sure buying leaves emergency savings and room for repairs.
      7. Add lifestyle value. Decide how much flexibility, control, stability, and responsibility matter to you.

      Common rent vs buy calculator mistakes

      • Comparing a small rental apartment with a much larger home purchase.
      • Using only the mortgage payment and ignoring other ownership costs.
      • Assuming home prices always rise.
      • Ignoring selling costs because they happen years later.
      • Using an investment return assumption that is too optimistic.
      • Assuming every renter invests the monthly savings difference.
      • Using a maintenance estimate that is unrealistic for the property.
      • Ignoring the risk of moving earlier than planned.
      • Buying with too little cash left after the down payment.
      • Treating a small estimated financial advantage as a certainty.

      Frequently asked questions

      How does the rent vs buy calculator work?

      It estimates cumulative renting costs and the economic cost of owning over your chosen period. Ownership cost includes mortgage payments, taxes, insurance, maintenance, purchase costs, fees, and selling costs, while the model credits the buyer with estimated net equity at the end.

      Does the calculator compare rent with only the mortgage payment?

      No. Mortgage principal and interest are only part of ownership. The tool separately includes taxes, insurance, maintenance, HOA or building fees, other owner costs, purchase costs, and selling costs.

      What does net home equity mean in the result?

      It is the estimated future home value minus the remaining mortgage balance and estimated selling costs. Actual sale proceeds can differ because property value, taxes, repairs, commissions, legal fees, and other costs vary.

      Should I include an investment return for the renter?

      Include it only if you realistically expect to keep or invest the cash that would otherwise be used for the down payment and purchase costs. Use a conservative assumption and remember that investment returns are not guaranteed.

      What if I do not know how much home prices will appreciate?

      Run multiple scenarios. Try low, moderate, and higher appreciation assumptions and pay attention to whether the recommendation changes. If the decision is highly sensitive to appreciation, uncertainty is an important part of the answer.

      What is a good maintenance percentage?

      There is no universal percentage. Property age, type, climate, systems, condition, recent renovations, and association responsibilities all matter. Use the best property-specific estimate available and test a higher-cost scenario.

      Is rent wasted money?

      No. Rent pays for housing and often transfers many repair and property-value risks to the landlord. Owners also pay costs that do not build equity, including mortgage interest, taxes, insurance, maintenance, and transaction expenses.

      Does this calculator tell me whether I can afford a mortgage?

      No. It compares two housing strategies. Mortgage qualification and affordability require a separate review of income, debt, emergency savings, lender rules, interest-rate risk, taxes, insurance, and other household expenses.

      Does this tool replace a mortgage adviser, real-estate professional, tax adviser, or lawyer?

      No. It is an educational calculator. Local financing rules, taxes, insurance, contracts, tenant rights, purchase costs, and property law can materially affect the decision.
