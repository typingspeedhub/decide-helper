What this refinance calculator is designed to show

A mortgage refinance is a replacement of one debt contract with another. The new interest rate matters, but so do the closing costs, the new loan balance, the new term, how quickly principal is repaid, and how long you actually expect to keep the loan.

This tool estimates current and new monthly principal-and-interest payments, monthly payment change, simple break-even time, lifetime interest, interest over your expected time horizon, and the remaining balance under each option. It also highlights term extension and other trade-offs that can make an apparently cheaper refinance less attractive.

      Stress-test the result before acting

      Run the calculator again with a smaller rate improvement, higher closing costs, and a shorter time in the home. A refinance is more resilient when it still looks reasonable under less favorable assumptions.

      How the mortgage refinance calculator works

      The calculator compares what happens from today forward if you keep your existing mortgage with what happens if you replace it with the proposed new loan.

      It does not compare the refinance with the original terms of your existing mortgage. That would be misleading because some of the original loan has already been repaid. Instead, it uses your current balance, current rate, and remaining term as the baseline.

      Monthly payment calculation

      The calculator uses a standard fixed-rate amortizing payment formula for principal and interest. Taxes, homeowners insurance, association fees, mortgage insurance, and escrow adjustments are not included because those items may not change in the same way as the loan itself.

      Monthly payment = P × r × (1 + r)^n ÷ ((1 + r)^n − 1)

      Here, P is principal, r is the monthly interest rate, and n is the number of scheduled monthly payments.

      What the refinance break-even point means

      The simple break-even period estimates how long monthly payment savings need to accumulate before they recover the refinance costs you pay upfront.

      Simple break-even months = upfront refinance costs ÷ monthly required-payment savings

      If upfront costs are 4,800 and the new required principal-and-interest payment is 200 lower, the simple break-even point is about 24 months.

      This is useful as a first screen, but it is not the entire decision. It can miss differences in principal repayment, loan term, financed costs, and remaining balance. That is why this calculator also compares those items separately.

      Why a lower monthly mortgage payment can be misleading

      A lower payment can come from two very different sources: a better interest rate or a longer repayment period. For example, replacing a mortgage with 14 years remaining with a new 30-year mortgage can reduce the payment dramatically even when the economic improvement is modest.

      Payment relief is not always the same as savings

      If the new mortgage extends repayment for many additional years, part of the lower monthly payment comes from paying principal more slowly. Compare the new term and the remaining balance, not only the payment.

      How financed closing costs affect the refinance

      Some refinancing costs can be added to the new mortgage balance. This reduces the cash you need at closing, but the cost does not disappear. It becomes additional mortgage principal and may generate interest for years.

      The calculator therefore separates upfront costs from financed costs. Upfront costs are used in the simple break-even calculation, while financed costs increase the starting balance of the new mortgage.

      How the expected time horizon changes the answer

      You may not keep either mortgage until final payoff. A move, home sale, another refinance, or accelerated payoff can end the comparison much earlier. Enter the number of years you realistically expect to keep the loan so the tool can compare interest and remaining balances over that period.

      If you expect to move in three years, a refinance with a five-year break-even period is difficult to justify from monthly savings alone. If you expect to keep the mortgage for ten years, the same loan has more time to recover its costs.

      Why remaining balance matters

      Two mortgages can have similar payments but leave you with very different balances after five or ten years. A refinance that restarts a long amortization period may improve cash flow while slowing principal reduction.

      The calculator displays the estimated remaining balance under both options after your selected time horizon. If the refinance has a meaningfully larger balance, that difference is an important cost of the lower payment.

      How to interpret the decision score

      The score is a structured comparison, not a lender recommendation. It gives positive weight to a meaningful rate reduction, a break-even period shorter than your expected time in the loan, lower interest cost, and a term that does not unnecessarily stretch repayment. It subtracts weight for high costs, weak payment savings, a break-even period beyond your likely stay, and major term extension.

      Use the score to expose trade-offs

      A strong score does not guarantee that you qualify for the loan or that refinancing is right for your circumstances. A weaker score does not mean a refinance can never be useful, especially when the primary goal is payment stability or necessary cash-flow relief.

      What to include in refinance costs

      Depending on the lender, property, loan program, and location, refinance costs can include lender charges, origination fees, appraisal, title-related charges, recording charges, settlement services, discount points, and other transaction expenses.

      Prepaid property taxes, homeowners insurance, and prepaid interest may also affect cash needed at closing, but they are partly timing items rather than pure transaction expenses. For a clean break-even comparison, try to separate true refinance transaction costs from amounts you would pay anyway.

      When refinancing may deserve serious consideration

      • The new interest rate is meaningfully lower after fees are considered.
      • The simple break-even point is comfortably shorter than your likely time in the loan.
      • The new term is similar to or shorter than the current remaining term.
      • The refinance lowers interest without leaving a substantially larger balance later.
      • You want to replace an adjustable or uncertain payment structure with a predictable fixed-rate loan.
      • The required monthly payment reduction would materially improve household cash flow.
      • You have compared several written offers using similar assumptions.

      When keeping your current mortgage may be better

      • You are likely to sell or refinance again before the break-even point.
      • The new rate is only slightly lower and transaction costs are high.
      • The lower payment comes mainly from restarting a much longer loan term.
      • Your current mortgage already has favorable terms.
      • The refinance adds substantial costs to the new balance.
      • You are close to paying off the current mortgage.
      • Closing costs would weaken your emergency reserves.

      A practical refinance stress test

      1. Raise the closing costs. Add a margin for fees that may be higher than the first estimate.
      2. Reduce the rate advantage. Test a slightly higher new rate.
      3. Shorten your expected stay. Ask what happens if you move earlier than planned.
      4. Compare a term close to your remaining term. Do not default automatically to a new 30-year mortgage.
      5. Test financed costs separately. See how adding fees to principal affects the new balance and interest.
      6. Compare the balance after five or ten years. A lower payment is less attractive if you owe much more later.
      7. Proceed only if the refinance still solves your original goal. The headline rate should not dominate the decision.

      Common mortgage refinance calculator mistakes

      • Entering the original mortgage term instead of the remaining term.
      • Ignoring closing costs because they are rolled into the new loan.
      • Calling the entire monthly payment reduction “savings.”
      • Comparing a new 30-year loan with a current mortgage that has far fewer years left.
      • Using an unrealistically long expected time in the home.
      • Ignoring discount points or lender credits.
      • Comparing quotes from different dates without considering market movement.
      • Assuming taxes and insurance will change exactly like principal and interest.
      • Treating a simple break-even calculation as a complete refinance analysis.
      • Using the calculator result as professional mortgage or financial advice.

      Example: lower rate with a similar term

      Suppose a homeowner has 22 years remaining and refinances into a 20-year mortgage at a meaningfully lower rate. The closing costs are moderate, the monthly payment falls, and the break-even period is about two years. If the homeowner expects to stay seven years, the refinance may look comparatively strong because the rate improves without substantially extending repayment.

      Example: lower payment caused mainly by a longer term

      Now suppose a homeowner has 14 years remaining but refinances into a new 30-year mortgage. The required payment may fall dramatically. However, the tool may show a much larger remaining balance after five or ten years and more lifetime interest. The lower payment can still be useful for cash flow, but it should not be confused with a pure cost saving.

      Example: moving before break-even

      If refinancing costs 5,400 upfront and lowers the required payment by 150 per month, the simple break-even period is 36 months. A homeowner who expects to move in about two years would not recover those costs through monthly payment savings alone.

      Information this calculator cannot know for you

      • The actual interest rate and fees a lender will offer after underwriting.
      • Whether your rate is locked or how long the lock lasts.
      • Your credit, income, debt-to-income, equity, appraisal, or qualification status.
      • Mortgage insurance rules or program-specific requirements.
      • Future interest rates or home values.
      • Tax consequences or deductibility of mortgage interest or points.
      • Property taxes, insurance, escrow adjustments, or association costs.
      • Prepayment penalties or other contractual charges on your current mortgage.
      • Whether another loan structure is legally or financially suitable for you.

      Frequently asked questions

      How does the mortgage refinance calculator work?

      It compares your current mortgage with a proposed refinance using your current balance, current rate, remaining term, new rate, new term, upfront costs, financed costs, extra payments, and expected time in the loan. It estimates payment, break-even, interest, and remaining balances.

      What is the refinance break-even point?

      The simple break-even point divides eligible upfront refinance costs by monthly required-payment savings. It estimates how long the payment savings need to recover those upfront costs.

      Does a lower monthly payment always mean I save money?

      No. A lower payment can result from extending the loan term. Compare the remaining balance, interest, and term as well as the monthly payment.

      Should I enter the original term of my current mortgage?

      No. Enter the number of years remaining today. Refinancing should be compared with what happens if you keep the existing mortgage from this point forward.

      What if I roll closing costs into the new mortgage?

      Enter those costs in the financed-cost field. The calculator adds them to the new loan balance instead of treating them as upfront cash.

      Are taxes and homeowners insurance included?

      No. The payment calculations focus on principal and interest so the old and new debt structures can be compared directly. Taxes, insurance, mortgage insurance, and escrow may need separate analysis.

      What if I make extra principal payments?

      You can enter extra monthly principal for both the current and proposed loan. The amortization comparison will reflect faster balance reduction and lower interest when those extra payments are mathematically possible.

      Is break-even enough to make the decision?

      No. Break-even is a useful first screen, but the new term, financed fees, remaining balance, total interest, rate risk, and your actual goal also matter.

      How much lower should the new rate be?

      There is no universal percentage-point rule. The answer depends on your balance, costs, remaining term, new term, and how long you expect to keep the mortgage.

      Does a high decision score mean I should refinance?

      No. The score is an educational comparison based on your inputs. It does not evaluate qualification, legal issues, taxes, lender quality, or your full financial situation.
