Should I Refinance My Mortgage?

Refinancing can reduce your interest rate, lower a monthly payment, shorten a loan term, or change the type of mortgage you have. But a lower advertised rate does not automatically mean a better deal. Closing costs, a restarted loan term, financed fees, equity, credit, and how long you expect to keep the home can change the result. This guide helps you compare the old mortgage with the new one as a complete financial decision.

Updated September 8, 2026 · Educational decision guide

The short answer

Refinancing may make sense when the new mortgage meaningfully improves your rate, payment, loan structure, or repayment timeline, and the expected benefit is large enough to justify closing costs and other risks.

Keeping your current mortgage may be better when the savings are small, fees are high, you may move soon, you are already far into a favorable loan, or the new mortgage lowers the payment mainly by stretching the debt over many more years.

The key question is not “Can I get a lower rate?” It is “Will the new mortgage leave me financially better off after costs, time, and risk are included?”

A quick decision rule

A refinance deserves serious consideration when the new loan solves a clear problem, the costs are transparent, the break-even period fits your plans, and the new repayment structure is genuinely better rather than merely cheaper this month.

Refinancing may be a strong fit when:

  • Your new rate is meaningfully lower after fees are considered.
  • You expect to keep the home and mortgage beyond the break-even point.
  • You can shorten the term without making the payment uncomfortable.
  • You want to replace an adjustable rate with predictable fixed payments.
  • The refinance removes an expensive loan feature or improves cash flow for a clear reason.
  • Your credit and equity allow competitive offers.
  • You have compared several written loan estimates.

Keeping the current loan may be better when:

  • You may sell or move before recovering the closing costs.
  • The new rate is only slightly lower.
  • Fees or points are unusually high.
  • The lower payment comes mainly from restarting a long loan term.
  • Your current mortgage already has favorable terms.
  • You would use home equity for discretionary spending without a repayment plan.
  • The refinance would leave too little emergency cash.

1. Define exactly why you want to refinance

“Rates are lower” is not a complete goal. Decide what you want the refinance to accomplish before comparing offers.

Common refinance goals include:

  • Lower the interest rate.
  • Reduce the required monthly payment.
  • Shorten the loan term and become mortgage-free sooner.
  • Move from an adjustable-rate mortgage to a fixed-rate mortgage.
  • Change from one loan structure to another.
  • Remove or reduce a mortgage-related cost when eligible.
  • Access home equity through a cash-out refinance.

These goals can conflict. A shorter term may reduce total interest but raise the monthly payment. A longer term may lower the payment but keep you in debt longer. Decide which outcome matters most.

Practical first step

Write one sentence before requesting quotes: “I am refinancing primarily to ______.” Then reject offers that do not clearly improve that goal after costs.

2. Understand your current mortgage before replacing it

You cannot judge a refinance without a clear baseline. Gather the current loan details and compare the proposed mortgage against what would happen if you simply kept paying the existing loan.

Collect these numbers

  • Current principal balance.
  • Current interest rate.
  • Remaining loan term.
  • Current principal-and-interest payment.
  • Whether the rate is fixed or adjustable.
  • Any prepayment charge or special condition that may apply.
  • Mortgage insurance or similar loan-related charges, if applicable.
  • Approximate home value and current equity.

Do not compare a new 30-year mortgage with the original terms of your old loan. Compare it with the remaining years and remaining cost of your current mortgage.

3. Compare the new loan as a complete package

A refinance offer contains more than an interest rate. Two loans with the same headline rate can have different fees, points, credits, terms, and total costs.

For each offer, record:

  • New loan amount.
  • Interest rate.
  • Annual percentage rate or comparable cost measure where provided.
  • Loan term.
  • Fixed or adjustable structure.
  • Monthly principal-and-interest payment.
  • Points and lender fees.
  • Estimated third-party closing costs.
  • Cash required at closing.
  • Costs rolled into the loan balance.
  • Any lender credit tied to accepting a higher rate.

Make sure offers are compared on the same day or within a short period when possible, because market rates can move.

4. Calculate the true cost of refinancing

Refinancing is not free simply because no check is required at closing. Costs can be paid in cash, financed into the new balance, or indirectly exchanged for a higher interest rate through lender credits.

Potential costs may include:

  • Loan origination or lender fees
  • Discount points
  • Appraisal
  • Credit report
  • Title-related charges
  • Recording or government charges
  • Attorney or settlement fees where applicable
  • Taxes or transfer-related charges where applicable
  • Other required third-party services

Prepaid interest, property taxes, and insurance funding can also affect the cash needed at closing, although some of these items are timing-related rather than pure refinancing expenses. Separate true transaction costs from amounts you would have paid anyway.

Effective refinance cost = lender/third-party transaction costs + points + relevant penalties + added cost from financed fees

“No-closing-cost” does not necessarily mean no cost

A lender may cover some upfront costs in exchange for a higher rate, or costs may be added to the loan balance. Compare the resulting payment and total borrowing cost rather than relying on the label.

5. Calculate the refinance break-even period

The simple break-even period estimates how long monthly savings need to accumulate before they recover the upfront transaction costs.

Simple break-even months = eligible refinance costs ÷ monthly payment savings

Example: simple break-even

Suppose refinancing costs $4,800 and reduces the monthly principal-and-interest payment by $200.

$4,800 ÷ $200 = 24 months

The simple break-even period is about two years. If you expect to sell the home in one year, the refinance is difficult to justify on monthly savings alone. If you expect to keep the mortgage for seven years, the savings have more time to exceed the costs.

This shortcut is useful, but it can miss important differences in principal repayment, loan term, financed fees, taxes, insurance, and opportunity cost. Use it as a first screen, not the only calculation.

6. Watch what happens to the loan term

One of the easiest refinance mistakes is resetting the mortgage clock without noticing the long-term effect.

Imagine you have 18 years left on your current mortgage and refinance into a new 30-year loan. The monthly payment may fall substantially, but you have also extended the scheduled repayment period by 12 years.

Useful comparisons include:

  • Current remaining term vs new term.
  • Payment on a new term close to the current remaining term.
  • Payment on a shorter term if affordable.
  • Total projected interest under each option.

A lower payment is not automatically a saving

If the payment falls because the balance is spread over many additional years, part of the improvement is simply delayed repayment. Distinguish rate savings from term extension.

7. Compare remaining interest, not just the new rate

A lower interest rate is valuable, but the dollar amount of interest depends on the loan balance and how long it remains outstanding.

Refinance value ≈ remaining cost of current loan − total comparable cost of new loan

A useful comparison estimates the principal and interest you would pay from today forward under both paths. When evaluating lifetime cost, do not count repayment of principal itself as a financing expense: principal is money borrowed that must be repaid under either option. Focus on interest, fees, points, and other incremental costs while also tracking how quickly the balance declines.

If you plan to keep the loan for only five years, compare the two options over five years rather than automatically using the full 20- or 30-year schedule.

8. Decide how much monthly cash flow matters

Sometimes refinancing is less about maximizing lifetime savings and more about making the household budget safer. A lower required payment can create breathing room for emergency savings, essential expenses, or unstable income.

A lower payment may be especially useful when:

  • Your current housing payment is putting pressure on the budget.
  • You need to rebuild emergency savings.
  • Your income has become less predictable.
  • The new loan reduces the payment without creating excessive long-term cost.

But if you can comfortably afford the current payment, refinancing into a much longer term solely to create extra spending money may be less attractive.

9. Consider home equity, credit, and qualification

The refinance terms available to you depend on the lender, loan program, property, income, credit profile, debt obligations, equity, and market conditions. The attractive rate you see advertised may not be the rate you actually qualify for.

Before relying on an estimate, check:

  • Your credit reports for errors where available.
  • Your recent income and employment documentation.
  • Your current debts and required payments.
  • Your estimated property value.
  • Your mortgage balance and approximate loan-to-value ratio.
  • Cash available for closing without exhausting reserves.

Do not make major credit or borrowing decisions during the refinance process without understanding how they could affect underwriting.

10. Fixed-rate vs adjustable-rate refinancing

A refinance can change not only the rate but also the type of rate risk you carry.

A fixed-rate refinance may be attractive when:

  • You value predictable principal-and-interest payments.
  • Your current adjustable rate may reset higher.
  • You expect to keep the mortgage for a long time.
  • The fixed rate and closing costs are acceptable.

An adjustable-rate refinance requires extra care when:

  • The initial rate is low but future adjustments could be significant.
  • You are uncertain how long you will keep the loan.
  • Your budget would struggle with a higher future payment.

For an adjustable-rate offer, understand the initial fixed period, adjustment frequency, index, margin, caps, and maximum possible rate or payment structure before comparing it with a fixed-rate alternative.

11. Treat cash-out refinancing as a separate borrowing decision

A cash-out refinance replaces your mortgage with a larger loan and gives you part of the difference in cash. This can make sense in some situations, but it converts home equity into new debt secured by the property.

Ask these questions before taking cash out:

  • What is the cash being used for?
  • Would I borrow this amount if it were not attached to my mortgage?
  • How much does the larger balance increase monthly and total interest?
  • Does the new mortgage replace a very favorable existing rate?
  • What happens to my equity cushion?
  • Can I repay the larger loan comfortably if income falls?

Your home secures the debt

Using mortgage debt to fund discretionary consumption can turn short-lived purchases into long-term secured debt. Evaluate the purpose of the cash separately from whether refinancing the original mortgage is attractive.

12. Understand points, lender credits, and rate trade-offs

Some offers let you pay more upfront for a lower rate through discount points. Others provide lender credits toward closing costs in exchange for a higher rate.

Point break-even ≈ extra upfront cost for lower rate ÷ monthly savings from that lower rate

If you expect to keep the loan for a long time, paying for a lower rate can sometimes be worthwhile. If you may refinance again or move soon, a higher upfront cost may not have enough time to pay back.

Compare at least one low-fee option and one lower-rate option when available. This makes the trade-off visible.

13. How long do you expect to stay in the home?

Your expected ownership period is one of the most important refinance variables. Closing costs need time to be recovered.

Moving soon makes refinancing less attractive when:

  • The break-even period is longer than your likely stay.
  • You expect a job relocation or major household change.
  • You are already considering selling or moving.
  • The refinance requires substantial points or upfront fees.

Do not assume you will stay 10 years simply because the refinance looks better at 10 years. Use a realistic time horizon and test an earlier move as a downside scenario.

14. Compare refinancing with simpler alternatives

Refinancing is not the only way to improve a mortgage situation. Depending on your goal, another option may involve fewer costs or less complexity.

Alternatives may include:

  • Keeping the current mortgage and making extra principal payments.
  • Keeping the loan and redirecting extra cash to higher-priority savings or debt.
  • Requesting information from the current servicer about available loan changes where appropriate.
  • Using a separate home-equity product rather than replacing the entire first mortgage, if borrowing is necessary and suitable.
  • Waiting for stronger credit, more equity, or more favorable market rates.
  • Reducing other household expenses instead of extending the mortgage term.

For example, replacing a very low-rate first mortgage with a much higher-rate cash-out refinance can be expensive even if you need access to equity. Compare the cost of disturbing the existing mortgage with alternatives.

Questions worth asking every lender

  • Is this rate locked, and if so, until when?
  • Which fees are controlled by the lender?
  • Are discount points included?
  • Are any lender credits included?
  • Which costs are estimates and may change?
  • Will any closing costs be added to the loan balance?
  • Is there a prepayment penalty or other early-exit cost?
  • What conditions must be met for this offer?
  • How would the payment change with a term closer to my current remaining term?
  • What is the total amount I would pay if I keep this loan for my expected time horizon?

Practical mortgage refinance examples

Example 1: clear rate reduction and long time horizon

A homeowner has 22 years left on a mortgage. A new loan with a similar repayment horizon offers a meaningfully lower rate. Closing costs are moderate, and monthly savings recover those costs in about two years. The homeowner expects to stay at least eight years.

This is a stronger refinance candidate because the rate improvement is not being created mainly by extending the term, and the expected ownership period is comfortably longer than the break-even point.

Example 2: lower payment created by restarting 30 years

A homeowner has 14 years left on the current loan. A refinance offer cuts the monthly payment substantially but starts a new 30-year mortgage.

The payment relief may be useful, but the homeowner should not call the full payment reduction “savings.” Much of it may come from stretching repayment over 16 additional years. A 15- or 20-year refinance should also be compared.

Example 3: moving before break-even

A refinance would save $150 per month but requires $5,400 in eligible transaction costs. The simple break-even is 36 months. The household expects a likely move in about two years.

Unless the refinance provides another important benefit, keeping the current mortgage may be more sensible because the expected stay is shorter than the break-even period.

Example 4: adjustable rate to fixed rate

A homeowner has an adjustable-rate mortgage approaching a reset. A fixed-rate refinance does not create dramatic immediate savings, but it removes uncertainty and produces a payment the household can comfortably plan around.

The value here is partly risk reduction, not just monthly savings. The homeowner should compare the cost of the fixed-rate refinance with the realistic range of future adjustable payments.

Example 5: cash-out refinance replaces a very favorable mortgage

A homeowner wants cash for a major project but currently has a low-rate first mortgage. A cash-out refinance would move the entire mortgage balance to a significantly higher rate.

The homeowner should compare this with alternatives that leave the favorable first mortgage intact. The cost of borrowing the extra cash includes the effect of repricing the existing balance.

Example 6: paying points for a lower rate

One lender offers a lower rate if the borrower pays additional points. The extra upfront cost would take six years of monthly savings to recover, but the homeowner is uncertain whether they will keep the loan that long.

A slightly higher rate with lower upfront cost may be the more flexible choice.

When refinancing is usually worth serious consideration

  • The new loan materially improves the interest rate or loan structure.
  • The break-even period is shorter than your realistic expected ownership period.
  • The new term fits your financial goal rather than automatically restarting the clock.
  • You understand all lender and third-party costs.
  • You have compared several offers using the same assumptions.
  • You can afford the closing costs without draining emergency savings.
  • The refinance reduces meaningful adjustable-rate or payment risk.
  • Your credit, income, and equity support competitive terms.

When keeping your current mortgage deserves serious consideration

  • You may move before the refinance reaches break-even.
  • Your current rate is already attractive.
  • The rate reduction is small relative to fees.
  • The new payment is lower mainly because the term is much longer.
  • You are close to paying off the existing loan.
  • Your current financial profile would lead to weak refinance terms.
  • The refinance would consume important cash reserves.
  • You are tempted by cash-out borrowing without a clear long-term benefit.

Common mistakes when deciding whether to refinance a mortgage

Mistake 1: focusing only on the interest rate

Fees, points, term, loan balance, and expected time in the loan can matter as much as the headline rate.

Mistake 2: treating the entire payment reduction as savings

A lower payment may partly result from extending repayment. Compare amortization and remaining balance, not just monthly cash flow.

Mistake 3: ignoring break-even

Closing costs need time to be recovered. A refinance can look attractive monthly but lose money if you sell soon.

Mistake 4: automatically choosing a new 30-year term

Compare a term close to your current remaining term before resetting the mortgage for decades.

Mistake 5: assuming “no-closing-cost” means free

The cost may appear through a higher rate, a larger balance, or another pricing trade-off.

Mistake 6: comparing offers from different dates without context

Mortgage markets move. Try to compare offers made under similar market conditions.

Mistake 7: draining emergency savings to close

A refinance should improve financial resilience, not leave the household unable to handle a repair or income interruption.

Mistake 8: using cash-out refinancing for short-lived spending

Long-term debt secured by the home is a poor match for many discretionary purchases.

Mistake 9: forgetting that financed fees still cost money

Rolling costs into the loan avoids upfront cash but increases the amount borrowed and can add interest.

Mistake 10: accepting the first lender offer

Rates, fees, points, credits, and underwriting terms can differ. Comparison is part of the decision.

A seven-step mortgage refinance decision framework

  1. Define the goal. Decide whether you want lower interest, lower required payments, faster payoff, rate stability, or access to equity.
  2. Build the baseline. Record your current balance, rate, payment, remaining term, mortgage type, and expected time in the home.
  3. Collect comparable offers. Request written estimates and compare rates, terms, points, credits, fees, and cash required.
  4. Calculate break-even. Estimate how long it takes monthly savings to recover transaction costs.
  5. Compare balances and interest over your real time horizon. Do not judge a 30-year refinance only by the first month's payment.
  6. Stress-test your plans. Ask what happens if you move earlier, income falls, or an adjustable rate changes.
  7. Choose only if the new loan solves the goal after costs. The refinance should remain sensible without relying on perfect assumptions.

Mortgage refinance checklist

Current mortgage

  • You know the current principal balance.
  • You know the current rate and remaining term.
  • You know whether the rate is fixed or adjustable.
  • You know the current principal-and-interest payment.
  • You have checked for any relevant prepayment or exit costs.

New mortgage

  • You know the new loan amount, rate, and term.
  • You understand all points and lender credits.
  • You know which costs are paid in cash and which are financed.
  • You know whether the quoted rate is locked.
  • You have compared more than one offer.

Financial test

  • You have calculated the simple break-even period.
  • You have compared the remaining current term with the new term.
  • You have compared costs over your expected time in the home.
  • You understand why the monthly payment changes.
  • You will retain adequate emergency savings.

Before closing

  • Your original goal is still being met.
  • The final terms match the offer you evaluated.
  • You understand any adjustable-rate features.
  • You have reviewed cash-out borrowing separately, if applicable.
  • You are not relying on pressure or an artificial “today only” deadline.

Frequently asked questions

When does refinancing a mortgage usually make sense?

It may make sense when the new loan creates enough interest savings, payment improvement, term improvement, or risk reduction to justify its costs, and you expect to keep the loan long enough to benefit.

How do I calculate the refinance break-even point?

For a simple estimate, divide eligible upfront refinancing costs by monthly payment savings. Then compare that result with how long you realistically expect to keep the mortgage.

Is a lower mortgage rate always a reason to refinance?

No. High fees, a much longer term, a short ownership period, or a small rate reduction can make refinancing unattractive even when the new rate is lower.

How much lower should the new mortgage rate be?

There is no universal percentage-point rule that works for every borrower. The required rate improvement depends on loan balance, fees, remaining term, new term, expected ownership period, and your goals.

Should I refinance to lower my monthly payment?

It can be useful for cash flow, but check whether the payment falls because of a lower rate, a longer term, or both. A lower payment is not automatically a lower total cost.

Should I refinance to a shorter mortgage?

A shorter term can reduce the time in debt and may reduce total interest, but the required monthly payment can be higher. It should fit comfortably within your budget.

Should I refinance an adjustable-rate mortgage into a fixed-rate mortgage?

It may be worthwhile when payment stability is important and the fixed-rate terms are reasonable after fees. Compare the current adjustment rules and possible future payments with the new fixed loan.

Can I roll closing costs into the new mortgage?

Some refinance structures allow certain costs to be financed. This reduces cash needed at closing but increases the amount borrowed and can increase interest paid over time.

What does a no-closing-cost refinance mean?

It generally means you are not paying some costs directly upfront. The lender may recover them through a higher interest rate or another pricing structure. Compare the total economics.

Should I pay discount points?

Paying points can make sense if the lower rate produces enough savings and you keep the loan long enough to recover the extra upfront cost. Calculate a separate break-even period for the points.

Should I refinance if I plan to move?

A short expected ownership period makes refinancing less attractive when closing costs cannot be recovered before the move. Compare your likely moving date with the break-even point.

Is cash-out refinancing the same decision as rate refinancing?

No. Cash-out refinancing adds a borrowing decision because you increase the mortgage balance and reduce equity. Evaluate the purpose and cost of the additional debt separately.

Final decision

Refinance your mortgage when the new loan clearly improves your financial position after closing costs, points, term changes, and your realistic ownership period are included.

Keep your current mortgage when the savings are too small, the break-even period is too long, the new term unnecessarily extends repayment, your current rate is already favorable, or you are likely to move before the benefits arrive.

Do not judge a refinance by the advertised rate or monthly payment alone. Judge it as a complete replacement of one debt contract with another: rate + balance + fees + term + monthly payment + total interest + risk + time in the home.

The strongest refinance decision is one that still looks sensible when you compare it with simply keeping the current loan and when you use realistic—not perfect—assumptions about how long you will stay.
